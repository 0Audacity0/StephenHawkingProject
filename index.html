What is a black hole?

A black hole is a region of space where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape from it. Black holes form when a huge amount of matter is squeezed into an incredibly small space, meaning stars going into supernova can become one. The boundary around it is called the event horizon, which is considered the point of no return. Anything that crosses it is pulled inward toward the center, called the singularity, where our current physics breaks down. Stephen Hawking showed that black holes are not perfectly black, because they slowly "leak" a faint glow now known as Hawking (wow almost like Stephen HAWKING) radiation.

What are the two types of black holes?

The two main types are stellar mass black holes and supermassive black holes. Stellar mass black holes form when a very massive star runs out of fuel, collapses, and explodes as a supernova. They usually hold between about five and a few dozen times the mass of our Sun. Supermassive black holes sit at the centers of galaxies and contain millions or even billions of Suns’ worth of mass. Astronomers also suspect rarer middle sized ones exist, but those two categories are the ones most people learn first.

What is an astrophysicist?

An astrophysicist is a scientist who uses the laws of physics to understand stars, planets, galaxies, and the universe as a whole. They study how objects form, how they change over time, and what they are made of. To do this they use telescopes, space probes, particle detectors, and powerful computer simulations. Some astrophysicists collect observations while others, called theorists, work mostly with mathematics. Stephen Hawking was a theoretical physicist and cosmologist who worked in exactly this territory, especially on black holes and the origin of the universe.

What is the Big Bang theory?

The Big Bang theory says the universe began nearly 14 billion years ago from an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding ever since. It was not an explosion into empty space, but an expansion of space itself. Early on, the universe cooled enough for the first atoms, which were mostly hydrogen and helium, to form. The theory is supported by the observed expansion of galaxies and by the cosmic microwave (yes microwave) background, a faint afterglow of light found in 1965. Stephen Hawking and others explored what could have happened at the very beginning, when gravity and quantum physics both mattered.

TON 618 is an ultramassive black hole that is about ten billion light years away from Earth. Estimates of its mass range from roughly 40 to 66 billion times the mass of the Sun. Its event horizon is tens of times wider than the orbit of Neptune, which is large enough to swallow our whole solar system.
